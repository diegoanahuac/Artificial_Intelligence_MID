**Who leads AI development.** The two clear leaders are the **United States** and **China**. The US
leads in frontier models and private investment: the Stanford AI Index 2025 counted 40 notable AI models
from US institutions in 2024, against 15 from China, and US private AI investment that year was about
twelve times China's. China is closing the gap quickly, though. Its best models now score close to the
US ones on the main benchmarks, it publishes more AI papers and files more AI patents than any other
country, and companies like DeepSeek showed that strong models can be trained at a much lower cost. The
race is less about one country winning and more about who controls the chips, the data centers and the
talent.

**What Mexico is doing.** Mexico is behind those two, but there are concrete steps. The federal Agencia
de Transformación Digital y Telecomunicaciones is putting government procedures online, with Llave MX as
a single digital identity, which is the base any public AI service would need [verify]. The government
also announced a national public supercomputer, *Coatlicue*, meant to train and run AI models for
research and public services [verify]. The weak points are the lack of a national AI law and the gap
in internet access between cities and rural areas.

**AI in five years.** I think AI will be part of most ordinary software: writing, search, customer
service and programming will all have an assistant built in, as the tools in Activity 18 already show.
I do not expect it to replace whole professions in five years, but I do expect it to change what entry
level jobs look like, because routine tasks will be done by the assistant and people will be paid for
checking its work, knowing the business and taking responsibility for decisions. The countries and
students who learn to use these tools critically, and not blindly, will get the most out of them.
